# DISCUSSION

## 1. Long-term nutrient enrichment altered leaf nutrient status but not integrated carbon–water relations

After 18 years of N and P addition in the NUMEX experiment, the studied species did not exhibit a significant increase in leaf intrinsic water-use efficiency (iWUE) relative to control plots — with the exception of *Pouteria torta* — despite detectable increases in foliar nutrient traits. This decoupling between nutrient accumulation and integrated carbon–water relations suggests that increased nutrient availability alone was insufficient to modify the long-term physiological regulation of carbon assimilation relative to stomatal conductance in the studied species.

The observed changes in foliar nutrient traits demonstrate that the fertilization treatment was effective. Primary productivity in tropical montane forests (TMF) is thought to be limited by nitrogen (N), or co-limited by N and phosphorus (Elser et al., 2007; González & Ostertag), and we therefore expected stronger responses under stronger limitation. In line with this expectation, species at 2000 and 3000 m responded strongly to nitrogen addition, with higher leaf nitrogen concentration (LNC), leaf phosphorus concentration (LPC), and area-based nitrogen content (Narea). LNC and LPC are widely used as consistent indicators of a response to increased nutrient availability ​(Báez & Homeier, 2018; Baldos et al., 2015; Dietrich et al., 2016; Firn et al., 2019; Homeier et al., 2012a, 2013b, 2017)​.

Narea generally increased after N addition, but a strong response was only detected for *Myrcia* sp. nov. and *Hedyosmum purpurascens*. Expressing leaf N on an area basis helped reveal that species at 2000 m maintain greater nitrogen investment per unit leaf area, highlighting the importance of structural effects in shaping foliar nutrient patterns. These results are consistent with previous work in the area on seedlings (Cárate-Tandalla et al., 2018) and mature trees ​(Homeier et al., 2012b, 2021)​. One possible explanation for the similar responses at 2000 m and 3000 m is the shared parent rock material, which may have led to similarities in community composition and leaf properties ​(Homeier & Leuschner, 2021b)​. Additionally, the high N:P ratios reported here and elsewhere suggest that nutrient stoichiometry is largely determined by species-specific nutrient acquisition and allocation strategies rather than by local edaphic conditions ​(Homeier et al., 2021)​.

Taken together, these results show that nutrient addition does influence foliar nutrient status in these mature montane forests, but that the response is filtered by species-specific acquisition strategies and by the shared geological context of the upper-elevation sites — and, critically, that this shift in nutrient status was not accompanied by a corresponding shift in leaf-level carbon–water relations.

**The exception of *Pouteria torta*.** *P. torta* was the only species to show a significant increase in iWUE under nutrient addition, and this exception is informative rather than incidental. Unlike the acquisitive-strategy explanation one might expect for a positive nutrient response, *P. torta* follows a conservative resource-use strategy similar to the other species in our dataset (see Pierick PCA), yet it occurs at the lowest elevation (1000 m), where warmer temperatures, reduced cloud immersion, and a distinct baseline nutrient limitation set it apart from the mid- and high-elevation species. This is consistent with our broader finding (Section 3) that species at 1000 m show a distinct relationship between leaf nutrient investment and iWUE compared to species at 2000–3000 m. Moreover, a significant N×P interaction has previously been reported for *P. torta* (XXX — cita), indicating that its growth and/or nutrient status respond synergistically to combined nitrogen and phosphorus availability rather than to either nutrient in isolation. This documented sensitivity to N×P interactions suggests that *P. torta* may be inherently more responsive to changes in nutrient supply than the other species studied here, regardless of its otherwise conservative strategy. Rather than treating this as an outlier, we interpret it as evidence that species-specific sensitivity to nutrient addition is not fully predicted by resource-use strategy alone: at least for this species, a documented history of N×P interaction combined with lower-elevation environmental conditions appears to override an otherwise conservative physiological profile. Identifying the traits — and the nutrient-interaction dynamics — that predict this kind of species-specific responsiveness remains an important direction for future TMF nutrient-manipulation research.

## 2. Nutrient uptake occurred, but increased nutrient availability did not translate into changes in leaf physiological performance

Contrary to our hypothesis, increases in foliar nutrient concentrations were not accompanied by changes in iWUE, δ¹³C, or Ci/Ca. Because δ¹³C integrates carbon assimilation relative to stomatal conductance over the period of leaf formation, this indicates that nutrient enrichment did not substantially modify the long-term balance between carbon gain and water loss in the studied species.

One possible explanation is that the added nutrients are being allocated to purposes other than photosynthesis. Under optimality theory, plants allocate resources to minimize the cost of acquiring the most limiting resource while maintaining carbon gain; if nutrient addition relaxes N or P limitation, the balance of constraints may shift toward other limiting resources such as water or light. Previous studies in tropical montane forest species (e.g., Dusenge et al., 2015; Ziegler et al., 2020) have shown that total nitrogen content — whether expressed on a mass or area basis — is not always a good predictor of photosynthetic capacity, largely because nitrogen allocation within leaves depends on a species' ecological strategy. Climax species, in particular, may allocate a greater proportion of nitrogen to structural components, defense, or light-harvesting complexes rather than to carboxylation machinery (e.g., Rubisco). Likewise, phosphorus may be allocated mainly to nucleic acids ​(Zhang et al., 2018)​. As a result, increases in LNC or Narea do not necessarily translate into proportional increases in photosynthetic capacity, because the added nutrients are being channeled elsewhere.

## 3. Conservative species strategies and environmental context likely buffer responses to nutrient enrichment

The species selected for this study are adapted to nutrient-poor soils and follow comparatively conservative ecological strategies (see Pierick PCA), which may be associated with inherently conservative water-use strategies. Ontogenetic effects may also play a role: mature trees have been shown to maintain stable water-use strategies over time ​(T. Yan et al., 2022a)​. In addition, trees at the upper site are exposed to persistent cloudiness and low-light conditions ​(Wittich et al., 2012)​, which may favor nitrogen allocation to light-harvesting rather than to carboxylation. Future work could usefully explore whether nutrient responses and allocation patterns diverge among species with contrasting ecological (acquisitive vs. conservative) strategies.

This buffering is also visible along the natural elevational gradient. Although long-term nutrient addition did not modify intraspecific iWUE, clear differences emerged between species across elevation: common species from the mid- and high-elevation sites exhibited the highest iWUE (Fig. S2), sharing more similar carbon–water traits with one another than with species at 1000 m. These differences likely reflect coordinated variation in nutrient availability, leaf functional traits, and community composition along the gradient. Previous work in the same study region reported maximum photosynthetic rates around 2000 m, coinciding with higher foliar nutrient concentrations (Wittich et al., 2012); consistent with this, we found greater nitrogen investment per unit leaf area (Narea) in mid-elevation species, which may contribute to their relatively high iWUE. In contrast, species at 1000 m displayed a distinct relationship between leaf nutrient investment and iWUE, indicating a different coordination between nutrient acquisition, leaf structure, and carbon–water exchange.

Together, these findings suggest that natural variation in nutrient availability across the elevational gradient is more strongly associated with leaf carbon–water relations than experimental nutrient enrichment is. Rather than responding plastically to increased nutrient supply, the dominant species appear to maintain conservative, species-specific physiological strategies. Because elevation, nutrient availability, and species turnover are inherently confounded in this study, future work incorporating a larger number of species across multiple elevations will be needed to disentangle the relative contributions of environmental filtering, nutrient availability, and species identity to patterns of water-use efficiency in TMF.

**Limitations.** Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. First, the absence of a statistically significant iWUE response does not necessarily imply the absence of a biological effect; given the number of species and replicates per elevation, statistical power may have been limited, and small or variable effect sizes could go undetected. Second, our design does not fully separate the effects of nitrogen and phosphorus addition, nor test for N×P interactions across all species, which limits our ability to determine whether co-limitation (Elser et al., 2007) was actually relaxed by the treatment — the documented N×P interaction in *P. torta* (Section 1) suggests this distinction may matter more broadly than our aggregated N/P framing captures. Third, because elevation, species identity, and ambient nutrient availability are confounded in this study, the elevational patterns in iWUE cannot be unambiguously attributed to nutrient availability alone. Fourth, δ¹³C integrates carbon assimilation and stomatal conductance over the period of leaf formation, which may obscure shorter-term or seasonal physiological adjustments not captured by this integrated proxy. Finally, our interpretation of nutrient allocation to structural or belowground sinks (Section 4) is inferential, as this study did not directly measure canopy structural traits, litterfall, or root and mycorrhizal responses to fertilization.

## 4. Nutrient responses may occur primarily through structural rather than leaf-level physiological adjustments

Rather than reflecting a leaf-level physiological adjustment, the added nutrients at our study sites may instead be channeled into structural and canopy-level growth — though we did not measure this directly and can only draw on comparable work elsewhere in this experimental network. Nutrient addition has been shown to increase foliage production in *Alchornea lojaensis* (Spannl et al., 2016), and to increase stand-level leaf area index and leaf lifespan under higher phosphorus availability (Homeier et al., 2013). If a similar structural response occurred in the species studied here, it would offer one plausible explanation for the lack of a leaf-level iWUE response: plants may prioritize producing a greater number of coordinated leaves over increasing the physiological performance of any single leaf ​(Lankhorst et al., 2025)​, and increased self-shading or litter accumulation in more structurally developed canopies could keep stomatal conductance stable even under elevated nutrient supply ​(T. Yan et al., 2022b)​. These mechanisms remain untested in our dataset, however, and confirming them will require pairing leaf-level physiological measurements with direct estimates of canopy structure — leaf area index, litterfall, and leaf lifespan — at the same plots used here.

## 5. Implications for tropical montane forests and ecosystem models

Despite evidence from the NUMEX experiment that nutrient addition alters foliar nutrient concentrations and several ecosystem processes in Ecuadorian TMF, the absence of a consistent iWUE response in the dominant tree species studied here indicates that carbon–water relations may remain strongly constrained. Such conservative carbon–water strategies may confer physiological stability under changing environmental conditions, allowing dominant species to maintain coordinated carbon assimilation and water use despite increased nutrient availability (Reich, 2014; Smith & Keenan, 2020). Consequently, future changes in carbon sequestration in TMF may depend more on shifts in forest structure, species composition, and leaf area than on enhanced leaf-level physiological efficiency.

These results are also relevant to current modeling frameworks, which may overestimate leaf-level physiological responses if they assume that nutrient alleviation automatically enhances carbon assimilation through changes in stomatal or photosynthetic functioning ​(Waring et al., 2023)​. We therefore conclude that predicting future forest dynamics in response to nutrient-induced changes in TMF species requires a better understanding of how nutrients are allocated, and of species' responses to other co-occurring limiting factors.
